## 1. Claude

**Short answer:** Rumi has plenty of competitors for the general job, which is teaching a young kid Spanish. I found almost none that make the same bets together. Those bets are: built for a child who can't read yet, audio first, native voices, word-by-word slow playback, and it works offline with no account. The closest match is Gus on the Go. Most of the rest are subscription platforms, video courses, or AI tutors made for adults.

**The field, in five groups**

| Group | Examples | How they teach | How close to Rumi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Picture-and-audio games for pre-readers | Gus on the Go, Studycat (Fun Spanish), Endless Spanish | Tap a picture and hear the word. No reading needed | **Closest.** Gus is a one-time purchase with no subscription. Studycat now markets itself as "AI-free and ad-free" |
| Subscription "everything" platforms | Lingokids, Dinolingo, ABCmouse (1,200+ Spanish activities) | Games, songs and videos, adaptive, with a parent dashboard | Same audience, opposite design: accounts, recurring billing, large content libraries |
| Video immersion courses | Little Pim (ages 0 to 6), Muzzy (BBC) | Short cartoon episodes. The child watches rather than interacts | Built on passive listening. Rumi asks the child to do things |
| Reading apps | Duolingo ABC | Phonics and tracing, with stories read aloud and each spoken word highlighted | **Copies a mechanic, not the job.** Duolingo ABC teaches kids to read English. Its highlight-each-word stories work like Rumi's turtle speed |
| AI voice tutors | ISSEN, Jumpspeak, Langua, Babbel Speak | Real-time conversation with a synthetic voice | Built for adults and teens. I found nothing made for a 5-year-old |

**Where Rumi actually stands apart**

1. **It runs offline as one file with no account.** Every subscription platform needs an account and a connection. Gus on the Go is the only competitor that comes close.
2. **The slow speed uses the real recording, one word at a time.** A slow speed usually either stretches the audio or reads the text with a robot voice. Rumi plays the native speaker's own recording word by word. That is hard to copy because it needs timing data for every word.
3. **English hints for a child who can't read.** The spoken English cues let a pre-reader get unstuck without asking a parent. Most apps in the first group give hints as pictures only.
4. **You pick from five native voices, Spain or Latin American.** Most kids' apps use one narrator.

**Risks and pushback**

- **"Different" is not a market.** Gus on the Go shows the offline, one-time-purchase model works as a product. It also shows the ceiling: a few dollars per language and little money for new content. The subscription platforms win on distribution and budget, and Rumi can't beat them on how much content it has.
- **An AI tutor for kids is the obvious next competitor.** Adult AI tutors already talk back in real time. When one of them builds a version for a 5-year-old, speaking practice gets cheap. Rumi's defenses are trust and working offline, not the tech. Studycat's "AI-free" marketing suggests parents already care about that.
- **The research has a gap.** Pricing and features come from 2026 review roundups, and many of those are written by competitors (Dinolingo, Studycat, Preply). I did not install or test any of the apps.

**So what:** Rumi looks less like a smaller Lingokids and more like a boutique product made for one child. The comparison is a small craft publisher versus Netflix: it wins on care and trust, not on volume. If it ever goes to other families, the positioning to test is "the calm, offline, no-account Spanish app for pre-readers." Gus on the Go is the one competitor worth studying closely.

## 2. Claude

**Your correction is half right.** Pre-literacy by itself is not unique. Gus on the Go, Studycat, Little Pim and Dinolingo all say kids don't need to read. What's actually open is pre-literacy combined with calm design. The calm-design market exists, and the language market exists. As far as I found, nobody sells both in one product.

**Why pre-literacy alone won't hold as a differentiator**
- Parents comparing apps will see "no reading required" on several competitors' pages. If Rumi leads with it, it looks like the others.
- There may still be a real gap underneath. Many "no reading" apps still use text for menus and navigation, or only teach tap-the-picture vocabulary. Your experience of hitting a reading wall is worth writing down in detail, as specific examples. Those examples are your pitch, and they tell you whether the gap is real or just in how the apps are marketed.

**The calm lane already has a proven model: Pok Pok**
- Pok Pok is a Montessori-inspired app for ages 2 to 7. It has no points, coins or streaks, and its pitch is that "kids naturally stop when they're done."
- It sells a subscription at $6.99 a month or $45.99 a year, and it has run a $59.99 lifetime deal. That shows parents will pay a premium for calm.
- It doesn't teach a language. So the one-line positioning is **"Pok Pok for Spanish."** It borrows a pitch parents already understand and puts it in a category it doesn't cover.
- The incumbent you'd be positioning against is Lingokids. Reviewers criticize it for heavy reward loops, like stickers and stars. One critique says kids can pass its games by tapping until something works, which rewards clicking over learning.

**Be careful with the ADHD framing**
- **Recommendation:** market it as "calm by design, built for kids who get overstimulated." Do not market it as "for ADHD."
- **Why:** "For ADHD" sounds like a health claim. That invites questions about evidence you don't have, and it shrinks the audience to one diagnosis. "Calm" reaches the ADHD parent and also the much larger group of parents who worry about screen time.
- **A design catch:** kids with ADHD often do need quick feedback. The goal is not less stimulation overall, it's no manipulation. Three concrete rules:
  1. Every response comes right away and matches what the child did. A correct word gets a warm voice line, not a burst of fireworks.
  2. No unpredictable rewards. That means no surprise loot, chests or streaks. This slot-machine mechanic is what actually hooks kids.
  3. Build in natural stopping points. A session ends with a closing moment, not an automatic next level.

**Accounts: agreed, with consequences to plan for**
- **Put the account on the parent, never the child.** A US law called COPPA restricts collecting data from kids under 13. A parent-only account with no child profile data keeps you mostly clear of it. Pok Pok advertises COPPA compliance for exactly this reason.
- **Drop "offline" as a selling point, but keep it working.** Keep the app working when the connection is bad. The only customer-facing sentence about it: "works on the plane."
- **Pricing to test:** copy Pok Pok's structure, a monthly plan, an annual plan, and a lifetime option. The lifetime tier fits a calm-app buyer, who is often a parent who distrusts subscriptions.

**So what:** Rumi's category is not Spanish apps. It's calm apps that also teach Spanish. Pok Pok shows the calm lane pays. Lingokids shows what parents are pushing back against. Pre-literacy is what shows the calm is real teaching and not just a sleepy app. It's supporting proof, not the headline.

## 3. Claude

**Recommendation:** Don't pitch Joe a business. Pitch him a demo. I'm assuming this is Joe Heitzeberg, founder of AI Tinkerers. His meetups screen for people who have actually built something and show working demos, not slides. He calls it "the Homebrew Computer Club of AI," after the 1970s hobbyist club where the Apple I was first shown. Your way in is the build story. The market story comes second, and only if he asks.

**Why that angle fits Joe**
- **He's a builder first.** Before AI Tinkerers he founded Crowd Cow (a meat delivery subscription), Snapvine and MediaPiston. He also co-founded Madrona Venture Labs, a startup studio that helps launch companies.
- **He'll dig into distribution and subscriptions.** Crowd Cow was a consumer subscription business, so he knows how hard it is to get and keep paying customers. Expect his questions to go there.
- **Rumi is contrarian about AI.** It's built with AI, but the child never chats with an AI. For a crowd drowning in chatbot demos, "I used AI to build the calm thing, not the addictive thing" stands out.

**The 30-second version** (for you to say out loud or adapt, not to paste anywhere)
> "My five-year-old can't read yet, and every Spanish app I tried assumed she could, or hooked her with slot-machine rewards. So I built Rumi. It's audio-first Spanish for kids who can't read, with no points, streaks or loot. Five native Spanish voices, and a slow mode that plays the real recording one word at a time. It's one HTML file with about 300 clips embedded, and I built it without being an engineer. Think Pok Pok, the calm kids app parents pay $46 a year for, but for learning a language."

Swap "she" for the right pronoun and check each fact before you say it.

**Demo flow, three minutes, on an iPad**
1. **The child's view (60 seconds).** Hand it over cold. Show that no screen needs reading.
2. **Turtle mode (30 seconds).** Play one line at normal speed, then word by word. This is the moment that makes people ask how it works.
3. **Under the hood (60 seconds).** Three things:
   - The whole app is one file.
   - The word-by-word timing came free from a Microsoft voice service. It returns the start time of every word, and the default tool was throwing that data away.
   - About 350 automated checks run on every change, including one that actually listens to the audio.
4. **The ask (30 seconds).** See below.

**What Joe will probably ask, and your answers**

| His question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| "Is this a business or a project?" | "It started as a project for my kid. The market signal is Pok Pok proving parents pay for calm, and nobody has done calm plus language." |
| "How do you get customers?" | Be honest that this is the open question. Name one channel to test, such as Spanish-immersion preschools or ADHD parent communities. |
| "Where's the AI?" | "In how it was built, and in the voices. It's deliberately not in front of the child." |
| "Why won't Duolingo just build this?" | "Their whole engine runs on streaks and reward loops. Calm goes against how they make money." |

**Risks**
- **Unclear ask.** Without a specific ask he'll be friendly and nothing will happen. Pick one before you reach out.
- **Leading with ADHD.** That turns a builder conversation into a clinical-claims conversation. Mention it only as "built for kids who get overstimulated."
- **Presenting it as an AI product.** It isn't one in the usual sense. Treat "no AI in front of the kid" as the hook, not something to apologize for.

**The one thing I need from you:** what do you want from Joe? A demo slot at an AI Tinkerers meetup, advice on the business, an intro to investors or a startup studio, or something else? The ask changes the opening line and the last 30 seconds. The rest of this holds either way.

**So what:** Joe's crowd rewards "look what I built and how." Lead with the child using it and the clever mechanics, and let the calm-app market thesis come out in his questions.
